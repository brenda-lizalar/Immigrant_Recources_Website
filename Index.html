Project Overview

Building Businesses, Facing Barriers: Mexican Immigrant Entrepreneurs

Mexican immigrants make up almost a quarter of the U.S. immigrant population and are deeply involved in small business ownership. This project focuses specifically on Mexican immigrant entrepreneurs—not immigration in general—and highlights the legal, financial, cultural, and social barriers they navigate when starting and growing businesses in the United States.

Goal of this site: Turn research on Mexican immigrant entrepreneurship into an accessible, public resource. The content draws from academic work on capital access, legal status, and Latino business ownership and connects it to real-world tools that entrepreneurs can use.

Section One

Barriers to Starting & Growing a Business

Research on Mexican immigrant entrepreneurship shows that many entrepreneurs have strong skills, networks, and work ethic, but face structural barriers that limit what they can do with those assets. Below are four key types of barriers, with examples of how they show up in everyday business life.

1. Legal & Immigration Status

Work authorization Licensing Fear of enforcement

Legal status shapes nearly every part of the business journey. Undocumented and mixed-status Mexican families may be blocked from professional licenses, formal employment, or federal loan programs.

  • Limited access to EINs, permits, and licenses can push businesses into the informal economy.
  • Fear of deportation or audits can discourage entrepreneurs from seeking advice or visibility.
  • Complex paperwork and changing policies create a constant climate of uncertainty.

2. Financial & Capital Barriers

Credit history Collateral Discrimination

Studies on Latino business ownership show that Mexican immigrant entrepreneurs often start with less startup capital than U.S.-born business owners and rely heavily on family savings.

  • Banks may deny loans due to thin or no credit history, informal income, or immigration status.
  • Without collateral, entrepreneurs turn to high-interest credit or predatory lenders.
  • Even when eligible, discrimination in financial services can lead to smaller loans or worse terms.

3. Cultural & Language Barriers

Business English U.S. bureaucracy Translating experience

Many Mexican immigrants bring rich entrepreneurial experience from markets, food stands, and trades in Mexico, but U.S. systems often only recognize formal credentials.

  • Contracts, leases, and loan applications are rarely available in plain Spanish.
  • U.S. tax rules, zoning laws, and health codes can be confusing without culturally responsive guidance.
  • Business workshops may not reflect the realities of migrant families working multiple jobs.

4. Social Barriers & Discrimination

Racism Neighborhood politics Policing of space

Discrimination doesn't only show up in laws—it also shapes who feels welcome to open a store on the "main street" and whose businesses are treated as temporary or less legitimate.

  • Negative stereotypes about Mexican workers can impact customer trust and landlord decisions.
  • Street vendors and food trucks may face over-policing or complaints that push them out of profitable areas.
  • Local politics can favor certain types of businesses while restricting immigrant-owned ones.

Section Three

Success Stories: Entrepreneurs in Context

These short case examples show how Mexican immigrant entrepreneurs use creativity, family networks, and community support to overcome some of the barriers described above. They are based on patterns discussed in scholarship and community reports, not on a single individual.

"María" – Taco Truck Owner
Central Valley, California

After migrating from Michoacán, María started selling tacos from a small stand with her cousins. At first she operated informally due to lack of permits and fear of enforcement. With the help of a local worker center and Spanish-language workshops, she obtained a food vendor license, a tax ID, and eventually a small business loan. Her truck now employs three workers from her community and advocates for fair street vending policies.

"Luis" – Construction & Remodeling
Dallas–Fort Worth Metro Area

Luis learned construction skills in Mexico and worked for years as a day laborer in Texas. Because he lacked credit history, banks initially denied him a business loan. By working with a community-based lender and building credit through a secured card, he was able to purchase tools and a work truck. Today he owns a small remodeling company that contracts with Latino homeowners and mentors younger workers just arriving from Mexico.

"Ana & Jorge" – Family Grocery Store
Chicago, Illinois

Ana and Jorge opened a neighborhood tienda that sells Mexican pantry staples and prepared foods. They faced rising commercial rents and skepticism from landlords. Joining a Latino Chamber of Commerce helped them negotiate a lease, understand city inspections, and apply for a small façade improvement grant. Their store now functions as a cultural hub where new immigrants find products, information, and community.

Section Four

How This Project Connects to Course Readings

Why entrepreneurship? Instead of focusing broadly on who is allowed into the U.S., this project centers on what happens after arrival—specifically, on Mexican immigrants who build businesses and participate in the U.S. economy. This shift responds to feedback that immigrant admissions alone is too broad and not grounded in course materials.

How readings shaped the site: Scholarship on Mexican and Latino business ownership highlights persistent gaps in access to capital, the impact of legal status, and the importance of community networks. These findings directly informed the four barrier categories and the resource lists on this website.

Course themes: The project connects to larger ETHN themes of racialization, labor, and resistance by showing how Mexican immigrant entrepreneurs both experience structural inequality and actively create alternative economic spaces—through taco trucks, mercados, family stores, and service businesses.

Selected Research & Data Informing this Site
  • Reports on Mexican immigrants in the U.S. that document their population size, labor market participation, and role in small business ownership.
  • Studies on Latino business ownership and capital access, which show that immigrant Latinos often start with less wealth and face harder paths to loans compared to non-Latino owners.
  • Research on undocumented entrepreneurs, street vending, and informal economies, which illustrates how legal status and local ordinances shape business strategies.
  • Ethnographic work on Mexican food trucks and markets, emphasizing how businesses double as cultural and political spaces in U.S. cities.
